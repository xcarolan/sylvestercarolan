I’ve always enjoyed history, so with the country’s 250th anniversary approaching, it seemed apropos to be reading about the history of America — the writing of the Declaration of Independence, for example (Jill Lepore’s article in The New Yorker is worth your time). The back-and-forth of the edits, and what the finished document meant to the nation and the world.

“We hold these truths to be self-evident, that all men are created equal.” That same June, Jefferson was also drafting a constitution for Virginia — a version that abolished slavery outright and declared that, in matters of inheritance, “females shall have equal rights with males.” Both provisions were edited out. What the founders wrote and what survived the editing are two different documents, and the gap between them is where the next 250 years happened.

In 1876, for the centennial, the Declaration was carried back to Philadelphia and displayed at Independence Hall. In 1952 it was moved to the National Archives under armed guard. Today the ink has faded so badly the signatures are barely legible — and some days the inspiration it once stirred seems to have faded with it.

One of the measures in my rubric is constitutional alignment: how faithful a candidate’s documented public conduct is to the constitution they’ll swear an oath to. Of the 240 candidates I’ve scored across 120 competitive races, 24 carry a DISQUALIFIED verdict — documented attempts to overturn certified election results, promotion of false fraud claims after courts had ruled, a federal bribery indictment, a resignation under threat of expulsion. Not inferences. Not vibes. Public conduct, on the record, measured against the oath they’re asking to take. Twenty-three Republicans and one Democrat — the rubric doesn’t check party registration, it checks conduct.

The words on the parchment faded from light and handling. The oath only fades if nobody’s checking.
