# Who Is Paying for North Carolina's Senate Race?

## The Question Behind the Question

Every Senate race is, at its core, a competition for representation. Who wins determines whose interests get weighed in Washington. Campaign finance records offer a second lens on that question — not as a substitute for evaluating candidates on the merits, but as a check: when a candidate's funding sources diverge sharply from the constituency they're asking to represent, that gap is worth naming.

The 2026 North Carolina U.S. Senate race between Roy Cooper and Michael Whatley presents one of the sharpest such divergences in the country this cycle. The data is drawn from FEC bulk filings for the 2026 cycle, covering individual contributions, committee contributions, and independent expenditures through the most recent reporting period.

## The Numbers at a Glance

| | Cooper | Whatley |
|---|---|---|
| Individual contributions | $6,597,755 | $2,483,835 |
| PAC contributions | $805,225 | $3,808,213 |
| Outside/independent spending | $630,167 | $3,083,213 |
| **Total** | **$8,033,147** | **$9,375,261** |

Whatley leads overall by about $1.3 million. But the composition of that lead tells a different story than the total does.

Cooper has raised nearly three times as much from individuals as Whatley has. Whatley's advantage comes entirely from PAC and outside money — $6.9 million of his total, compared to $1.4 million for Cooper.

## Where the Individual Money Comes From

Of Cooper's $6.6 million in individual contributions, $3,494,164 — 53 percent — comes from North Carolina donors, including 5,501 unique contributors. Whatley has raised $1,113,802 from North Carolina donors: 1,212 unique contributors. Cooper has more than four times as many in-state individual donors.

This is the most direct measure of grassroots support from the constituency both men are asking to represent. By that measure, Cooper's individual donor base is larger, more geographically rooted in North Carolina, and more broadly distributed.

Whatley's individual donor profile skews toward national private equity and finance. His top employer categories by total dollars raised: Blackstone ($34,000, nine donors), KKR ($20,000, four donors), Elliott Investment Management, Crestview Partners, Audax Group, and Coinbase. RDV Corporation — the holding company of the DeVos family — contributed through two donors. Louis DeJoy, the former Postmaster General who oversaw controversial slowdowns in mail processing ahead of the 2020 election, contributed $7,000 from Greensboro, North Carolina.

Cooper's top employer categories: Duke University ($40,136, fifty donors), UNC Chapel Hill ($18,274, nineteen donors), several Raleigh and Charlotte law firms, and a smaller presence from financial services. Both candidates received corporate donations from Blackstone; Cooper's total from that firm was $10,500 from three donors.

## The PAC Landscape

Cooper's $805,225 in PAC money comes primarily from the Democratic Senate caucus infrastructure — the DSCC ($62,000), a large cluster of Democratic member-to-member PACs at $10,000 each, and organized labor: the American Federation of Teachers, AFGE, AFSCME, the Teamsters, the Machinists, the International Association of Firefighters, the Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers, the Carpenters, and the Communications Workers of America, among others. Reproductive rights organizations — Planned Parenthood's federal PAC, Reproductive Freedom for All, and the American College of OB-GYNs' PAC — contribute at the $5,000 level. The League of Conservation Voters Action Fund ($7,430), JStreetPAC ($38,425), and the AAJ PAC (trial lawyers, $10,000) round out the major contributors.

Whatley's $3,808,213 in PAC money begins with two items that dwarf everything else: AFP Action at $2,344,221 and SLF PAC at $617,770. Together, those two committees account for $2,961,991 — 78 percent of his total PAC and outside spending.

The remainder of Whatley's PAC base is a comprehensive cross-section of corporate America. The U.S. Chamber of Commerce ($10,000), Caterpillar ($10,000), Truist Financial ($10,000), PNC Financial ($10,000), Pinnacle West Capital ($10,000), and Koch PAC ($7,500) represent the financial and industrial corporate sector. The fossil fuel industry is organized and present: Devon Energy, Marathon Petroleum, Phillips 66, Valero Energy, Continental Resources, Ovintiv, TransCanada, the American Petroleum Institute, and the U.S. Energy PAC (American Exploration & Production Council) all contributed. CoreCivic, the private prison company, gave $5,000. Salem Media Group — the operator of right-wing Christian radio stations — gave $3,500. LifePoint Health, a hospital chain owned by private equity, gave $3,500.

## The Two Entities Deciding This Race

AFP Action — formally Americans for Prosperity Action, Inc. — is the political arm of the donor network built by Charles Koch. It operates as a "cooperating committee," a legal structure that permits closer coordination with campaigns than a Super PAC. AFP Action spent $2,344,221 in independent expenditures for Whatley across fifteen separate transactions, and $64,000 in independent expenditures against Cooper in two transactions. This is by far the largest single outside investment in the race.

SLF PAC — the Senate Leadership Fund — is the principal outside spending vehicle of Senate Republican leadership, most closely associated with Mitch McConnell. SLF spent $617,770 in independent expenditures for Whatley across eight transactions, and $449,341 in independent expenditures against Cooper across seven transactions. Its total involvement in this race is $1,067,111.

Combined, AFP Action and SLF PAC have committed $3,025,992 to this contest — $2,961,991 for Whatley and $513,341 against Cooper. These two entities alone represent more than 32 percent of the total money in the race.

Cooper's outside spending picture is, by contrast, modest and largely hostile. His $630,167 in outside spending is dominated by money spent against him — $449,341 by SLF PAC, $64,000 by AFP Action, and $9,400 by Red Senate, a Super PAC. Money spent in his favor by outside groups totals approximately $108,000: JStreetPAC ($38,425 in direct contributions), ModSquad Action ($15,000), Field Team 6 ($8,727), the League of Conservation Voters Action Fund ($7,430), and smaller amounts from environmental and reproductive rights organizations.

## What This Means for Constituent Representation

The constitutional case for elected representation rests on accountability: representatives who face consequences from the people they serve are structurally more likely to act in those people's interests. Campaign finance doesn't determine how a senator will vote, but it does illuminate whose calls get returned, whose priorities get weighted, and whose judgment the candidate has implicitly invited to shape their political career.

Whatley will arrive in the Senate — if he wins — having received $2.3 million from a political network built by one of the country's largest fossil fuel and industrial conglomerates, and $617,000 from a vehicle controlled by national Republican leadership. He will have received money from CoreCivic (private prisons), from multiple oil and gas companies with active federal regulatory interests, and from the private equity firms whose financial engineering has reshaped industries that employ working North Carolinians. His individual donor base in North Carolina — 1,212 people — is smaller than the enrollment of some high schools in the state he's asking to represent.

Cooper will arrive having raised the majority of his money from 5,501 North Carolinians — the largest single donor category by both count and dollars — along with organized labor, academic institutions, and advocacy organizations aligned with voting access, reproductive rights, and environmental protection. The outside groups backing him are outraised nearly 30-to-1 by the outside groups backing his opponent.

These are not abstract observations. They are a description of the competing claims on a senator's attention, and on the question of who, exactly, the office will be used to serve.
